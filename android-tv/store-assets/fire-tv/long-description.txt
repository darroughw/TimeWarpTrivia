Six decades of pop culture trivia on one shared screen. Nobody leaves this game feeling great about their memory.

HOW IT WORKS

Launch TimeWarp Trivia on your Fire TV and a room code shows up. Everyone else takes out their phone, goes to timewarptrivia.com/play, types in the code and a nickname, and joins. No app store, no download, no account to make and immediately forget the password to. Anywhere from 2 to 10 people can be in the same room, which is also the range of people who will remember this later.

Once everyone's in, the host picks the decades in play: one, or all six of them, 60s through 2010s. Each round puts a question on the TV with a countdown clock, and everyone answers from their own phone before time runs out. Getting it right isn't enough. TimeWarp Trivia scores on speed, so a correct answer that arrives a half-second late loses to a wrong guess that arrives faster. The clock does not care about your reasoning process.

After the main rounds, whoever's currently in last place gets a bonus question, alone, on the big screen, while everyone else in the room watches in silence and offers no help whatsoever. It is, structurally, a public audition for the role of "person who lost."

WHAT'S PLAYABLE

Six decades of music, movies, TV, toys, and trends that used to be current events and are now trivia categories, which is its own small tragedy. Plus Deep Cuts: topic packs (The West Wing, Fallout, more coming) for the specific kind of person whose entire personality is one television show or one video game, and who will absolutely win that round and mention it again at dinner.

Thousands of questions across every category, so decade combinations don't repeat the same way twice. This is not a game you play once, lose interest in, and let quietly rot on a home screen.

BUILT FOR THE ROOM, NOT THE SCREEN

Nobody but the host installs anything. That's on purpose: this needs to work for whoever's actually in the room, including the relative visiting for one weekend who is not creating an account for your trivia game, and the friend who still has 4GB of storage free because they refuse to delete anything.

The scoreboard remembers every round, not just the last one, so a strong finish doesn't erase a weak start. People tend to remember it the same way.

No login. No account. No data entry beyond a nickname you'll regret picking two rounds in. Start a game, hand out the code, and watch what a room full of confident adults does when the clock starts.
